# Contact

Finding who to write to about a posting or a news lead, and how to reach them. The deliverable is
first a recommendation in chat — the person, why them, and the way to reach them — and, once the
user settles on it, the message drafted there and left unsent; invariant 1 covers Send as much as
Submit.

## About what

A posting key, or a company with a news lead. **A company with more than one posting
`shortlisted`, `staged`, `applied` or `interviewing` is asked about, by title.** A person named
instead is written to for the purpose the invocation states; with none, ask.

**A name alone matches several companies**, and the wrong one returns real people who work somewhere
else — nothing about the result looks wrong. Confirm it against the website the posting or the
article names. Where the two cannot be matched, say so rather than choosing the nearest.

**Where the posting came through a staffing firm, the company is the client**, if the description
names one. The firm's own staff are not a way in.

## Who

**One person, two at most, chosen now from why.** Nobody is stored: the right person changes as
people join and leave.

| Why | Who |
| --- | --- |
| A posting | The hiring manager — whoever leads the team the role sits in. The posting sometimes names them. The recruiter on it where no hiring manager can be told apart |
| A news lead | A founder, or whoever leads engineering |

**A 1st-degree connection at the company is a way in worth naming beside them** — the company's
LinkedIn page says how many work there. They are asked to refer or introduce, not to hire.

**Open only the profile of the person chosen.** LinkedIn tells a person who viewed their profile, so
a profile opened while looking is the user turning up in a stranger's notifications at a company
they are waiting to hear from. Choose from search results and the company's People tab, which show
name and title.

**LinkedIn restricts accounts that browse like a script, and the account is the user's own.** One
tab, opened by this run and reused; list pages only, and no further down one than its first two
pages. A sign-in page is theirs to get past: ask, naming the tab, and wait.

## How to reach them

**The way that holds the whole message and that this person reads.** Look in this order, and stop at
the first that holds:

1. **An email address published for them**, or for this kind of inquiry — the company website's
   team, about or contact page, their own site, the posting. An address for anyone, `info@`, is a
   way into a news lead, never into a posting.
2. **X, where they post.** A DM, where their DMs are open.
3. **LinkedIn**: Message for a 1st; otherwise Connect, with a note.

**An email address is never guessed and sent to by you.** With none published, list the likely
ones in order, first name at the company's domain first, for the user to try; a bounce is the test.

## Bring it to the user

Under the opening sentence: the person, their title, why them, the way to reach them and where it
was found; anyone else worth weighing, a line each. **Then stop.** Whether to write, to whom, and
which way, is decided with the user before anything is drafted.

## Writing it

For a news lead, read `references/news.md`, **Writing to a lead**, first.

| Way | Where the message goes |
| --- | --- |
| Email | Drafted in chat with a subject line, for them to send from their own mail |
| X | Typed into the DM box |
| LinkedIn, a 1st | Message, on their profile |
| LinkedIn, anyone else | Connect, then the note LinkedIn offers to add to the request |

**A connection note is capped at a few hundred characters, and LinkedIn counts them.** Write to the
count rather than letting it cut the ask off the end. Free accounts get a handful of notes a month:
when LinkedIn says they are used up, stop and say so.

**A Message button that opens InMail or a Premium offer is not a way in.** Never spend a credit or
start a trial.

**Read the thread before writing to anyone the user has written to before.** It is the only record
of how well the two know each other, and a message that introduces the user to someone they wrote to
last month reads as a mail merge. If the thread already holds an unanswered ask for the same thing,
stop and say so rather than adding a second.

### About a posting

**The match**: the title as the posting spells it, with the posting's URL where the box has room.
Then what the posting asks for most and the one or two things in the record that meet it — by the
same rule as a fit question in `references/applying.md`, not the resume retold.

**Say where the application stands, as the posting's `status` has it** — in already, or about to go
in. Some employers only take a referral made before the application, so the reader needs to know
which.

**The hiring manager or a recruiter is asked only to look at it**: a referral goes to them. Anyone
else at the company is asked for a referral if, having read the match, they think the fit is real.

### What it says

**Path, Proof, Ask. Under 100 words**, in this order:

1. **Why this person** — the path: the mutual connection by name, the school, as the profile shows
   it. It is the reason a stranger reads the second sentence. Never claim an acquaintance the
   profile and the thread do not show; someone the user knows needs no path at all, and a stranger
   with none opens on the purpose instead.
2. **One line of proof**: the single thing in the user's record that meets the purpose. One, even
   where the record holds five.
3. **The ask is a conversation, not a job**: their view of the problem that proof sits in, and where
   someone who built it could be useful. A posting keeps its own ask, above.
4. **A question they can answer yes or no, with a timeframe**: fifteen minutes in the next couple of
   weeks.

**Nothing about them the profile does not show.** Praise for a career or a post is the mark of mass
outreach, and the reader has seen it that morning.

**Cordial and professional**: a greeting by first name, a polite ask, and a sign-off of "Best," and
the user's first name on their own lines; an email adds `identity.linkedin` on the line under the
name. A connection note's sign-off counts against the cap, so the middle is the first thing cut to
make room.

## Hand it over

Type it into the box and stop, with the tab left open. Under the opening sentence, the message as
typed, so they can ask for a change without leaving the conversation; a change is retyped in the
same box.

## Adding a quirk

What a site turned out to need — where Connect hides, what a box refuses — earns a line here once
it is driven.

- **The note box's label says 300 characters; its counter, and the real cap on a free account, is
  200.** Write to the counter. Newlines count.
